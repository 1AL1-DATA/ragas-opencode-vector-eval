\begin{abstract}
LLM-as-a-judge is the default method for evaluating retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) systems, but it is slow, non-deterministic, and expensive.
We measure how well cheap, deterministic shortcuts---built from embeddings,
cross-encoders, natural language inference, and lexical overlap---predict the
rankings of four RAGAS judge metrics on a production-shaped corpus: the full
session store of an agentic coding tool mirrored into a local vector database.
Over 99 joined samples, AnswerCorrectness is strongly predicted by a
ROUGE-L plus embedding-cosine blend (Spearman $\rho=0.89$), and
AnswerRelevancy by query/document cosine using the index's own embedding
family ($\rho=0.71$). Faithfulness agreement is weakly negative
($\rho=-0.18$): the judge saturates at a median of 1.000 while a sentence-level
NLI shortcut reads a median of 0.000. ContextPrecision shows no
discriminative signal on this corpus ($|\rho|<0.13$ across all variants).
We conclude that shortcut-versus-judge agreement is a fast, actionable
diagnostic: it identifies which judge metrics can be replaced by deterministic
scores for ranking, and which judge metrics themselves require an oracle audit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is evaluated predominantly through
LLM-as-a-judge frameworks such as RAGAS~\cite{es2023ragas}. These metrics
require a capable judge model, are non-deterministic, and take hours to run
on realistic datasets with local models. Teams that iterate on retrieval
pipelines need cheaper, deterministic signals for regression gates and
system tuning.

In this work we ask an operational question: \emph{on a given corpus, which
RAGAS judge metric can be predicted by which cheap shortcut, and which judge
metrics are themselves unreliable?} Rather than a synthetic benchmark, we
evaluate on the session history of an agentic coding assistant---thousands of
real conversations---mirrored into a local vector database. Queries are
genuine user messages, responses are fresh generations conditioned on
retrieved chunks, and references are the historical assistant replies.

\section{System under evaluation}\label{sec:system}

The retrieval system is a derived vector index of a SQLite session store.
Messages are extracted from materialized tables into a JSONL corpus, chunked
to roughly 1400 characters with overlap, embedded with
\texttt{nomic-embed-text} (768-dimensional, cosine), and stored in a Chroma
collection. Retrieval is top-$k$ cosine search ($k=5$); a local generator
(\texttt{gemma4:12b}, temperature $0$) answers given the retrieved chunks.
The same model family serves as the RAGAS judge.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset}\label{sec:dataset}

We sampled 100 query/response/context triples deterministically (seed 42,
at most 5 per session) from the session store. Queries are user messages of
15--1500 characters; references are the historical assistant replies;
retrieved contexts are the top-5 chunks for the query; responses are fresh
generations conditioned on those chunks. After filtering invalid judge
outputs, \textbf{99} samples were joined across all analyses.

\subsection{Reference (judge) metrics}\label{sec:ragas}

We use the classic RAGAS metric classes~\cite{es2023ragas}: Faithfulness,
AnswerRelevancy, ContextPrecision, and AnswerCorrectness, run with a fully
local judge (\texttt{gemma4:12b} via Ollama) and \texttt{nomic-embed-text}
embeddings, single worker, on the GPU. The full pass took 8~h~26~min for the
400 samples (4 metrics $\times$ 100).

\subsection{Shortcut metrics}\label{sec:shortcuts}

All shortcuts are deterministic and run locally:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{ContextPrecision} $\rightarrow$ graded NDCG. A binary
  threshold variant collapses (about 89\% of chunks are lexically relevant),
  so relevance is graded: $\sigma(\mathrm{cross{\text{-}}encoder}(reference,
  chunk))$, and the metric is NDCG over graded relevance under the
  query-vs-chunk reranker order (retrieved and reranked variants; bge-reranker
  v2-m3 and ms-marco-MiniLM).
  \item \textbf{Faithfulness} $\rightarrow$ sentence-level NLI. Sentences of
  the response are split; each is entailed if
  $\max_c P_{\mathrm{entail}}(c,s) \ge 0.5$ (DeBERTa-v3 MNLI);
  score $=$ entailed $/$ total.
  \item \textbf{AnswerRelevancy} $\rightarrow$ asymmetric QA embeddings.
  TAS-B dot product, and nomic cosine with
  \texttt{search\_query:}/\texttt{search\_document:} prefixes.
  \item \textbf{AnswerCorrectness} $\rightarrow$
  $\mathrm{score} = 0.75\,\mathrm{ROUGE\text{-}L\,F_1}(\mathrm{response},
  \mathrm{reference}) + 0.25\cos(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{ref})$ with
  \texttt{nomic-embed-text}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Agreement analysis}\label{sec:agreement}

For each metric pair we compute Pearson and Spearman correlation over the 99
joined samples. Verdict bands: $|\rho|\ge 0.7$ STRONG, $0.4\le|\rho|<0.7$
MODERATE, $|\rho|<0.4$ WEAK. Spearman is primary because TAS-B is on an
unbounded scale.

\subsection{AnswerCorrectness is replaceable}\label{sec:ac}

The blend $0.75\,\mathrm{ROUGE\text{-}L} + 0.25\cos$ reproduces the judge's
ranking almost perfectly ($\rho=0.894$); each component alone is nearly as
good ($0.870$, $0.875$). For ranking and regression gating, any variant
suffices; the judge adds value only where absolute calibration matters.

\subsection{AnswerRelevancy: embedding family matters}\label{sec:ar}

The shortcut built from the retrieval index's own embedding family
(\texttt{nomic-embed-text}) with task prefixes agrees STRONGLY
($\rho=0.709$). The fine-tuned TAS-B encoder is MODERATE ($\rho=0.576$) and
numerically saturated.

\subsection{Faithfulness: the judge saturates}\label{sec:ff}

The judge's median is 1.000 (everything is faithful) while the NLI shortcut's
median is 0.000 (nothing is entailed by the retrieved chunks). With a
bimodal, near-degenerate judge, the correlation ($\rho=-0.185$) is driven by a
few low-score outliers. Both measurements are plausible; neither can be
trusted without a human oracle audit of 20--30 sentence-level entailment
decisions.

\subsection{ContextPrecision: no signal on this corpus}\label{sec:cp}

All variants are near-constant (bge NDCG mean $\approx 0.99$, std $\approx
0.01$; RAGAS median 0.70). Every retrieved chunk is relevant on this corpus,
so the metric cannot rank systems. This is a corpus degeneracy, not a general
failure of the metric.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Cheap wins for ranking.} Two of the four judge metrics are reliably
predicted by deterministic shortcuts that run in minutes rather than hours.
This enables CI gating on the shortcut with periodic judge-based calibration.

\paragraph{Disagreement is a diagnostic.} A near-zero correlation identifies
either a judge artifact (faithfulness ceiling) or a corpus artifact
(context precision degeneracy). Both findings are actionable and are surfaced
at the cost of one short computation.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} A single judge and generator (both
\texttt{gemma4:12b}) may inflate agreement relative to a frontier judge; the
TAS-B scale limits inference to rank statistics; and the corpus has a ceiling
on context relevance that may not generalize to other domains.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

On this real agentic-RAG corpus, AnswerCorrectness and AnswerRelevancy can be
evaluated cheaply and deterministically; Faithfulness is currently undecidable
without an oracle audit; and ContextPrecision carries no discriminative power.
The shortcut-versus-judge agreement check is cheap enough to run before
adopting any RAG metric on a new corpus.
